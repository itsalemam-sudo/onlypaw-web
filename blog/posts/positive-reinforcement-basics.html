Positive reinforcement: the only training method science actually supports

Published June 2026 · 7 min read · Filed in Training

If you've watched enough TV, you've seen a trainer yank a leash, pin a dog on its back, or lecture an owner about being the "alpha." Every major veterinary behavior body — the American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior, the British Small Animal Veterinary Association, the World Small Animal Veterinary Association — has now issued a position statement against those methods. The evidence is not close. Here's what works instead, and how to use it in your own home.

Why "alpha" theory is dead

The wolf-pack "dominance" idea comes from one 1947 captive-wolf study. In 1999, the study's own author, Dr. David Mech, publicly retracted the "alpha" framing after decades observing wild wolves — where packs are simply family groups led by parents. Dogs are not wolves, and even wolves don't behave the way TV trainers claim. Force-based methods don't teach the dog what to do; they teach the dog that you are unpredictable, which increases anxiety-driven aggression.

What positive reinforcement actually is

Add something the dog wants — food, praise, a toy, a scratch behind the ear — immediately after they do the thing you want. "Immediately" is not a suggestion; the reinforcement has to happen within about one second, or the dog will not connect it to the behavior.

The four cues every dog should know

Sit

  1. Hold a small treat between your fingers, at the dog's nose.
  2. Slowly move it up and slightly back over their head. Their nose follows, their bum drops.
  3. The instant their bum touches the floor: mark ("yes!" or a clicker) and deliver the treat.
  4. After 5–10 reps, add the word "sit" one second before the lure. After 20–30 reps, drop the lure.

Stay

  1. Ask for a sit. Say "stay." Wait one second. Mark and treat.
  2. Next rep: two seconds. Then three. Build duration before distance.
  3. If they break, don't scold — just reset silently. You went too fast; make the next rep easier.

Come (recall)

  1. Start indoors. Say the dog's name + "come!" in a happy tone.
  2. When they orient toward you, back away quickly — dogs chase movement.
  3. When they reach you: massive reward. Not "good dog" — a jackpot of five treats in a row, or a 30-second tug game.
  4. Never punish a dog who comes to you, ever. Even if they've been chewing your shoes for 20 minutes. Punishment on arrival poisons the cue.

Leave it

  1. Put a treat in a closed fist. Present it to the dog. They will lick, paw, mouth.
  2. The moment they stop trying — even a half-second pause — mark, open your other hand and give a different treat.
  3. Progress to open palm, then treat on the floor covered by your foot, then in the open on the floor.

Common mistakes

  • Treats too big. Pea-sized pieces. You want 30 reps per session, not 5.
  • Cue before the behavior is fluent. Don't add the word until the dog reliably does the motion.
  • Nagging. "Sit. Sit. Sit sit sit." Say the cue once. If nothing happens, reset — the cue isn't trained yet.
  • Only training in the kitchen. Behaviors don't generalize automatically. Practice in the hallway, garden, park, on-leash, off-leash. Each is essentially a new context to the dog.

When to bring in a professional

If your dog shows aggression toward people or other dogs, hire a certified force-free trainer — look for credentials like KPA-CTP, CPDT-KA, or IAABC. A confrontational trainer will make a fearful or reactive dog measurably worse.
